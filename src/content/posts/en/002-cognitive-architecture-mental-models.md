## Introduction: Patterns in the Labyrinth of Cognition

One concept that has deeply inspired me lately is **mental models**. They have opened my eyes to how humans develop and adapt to a changing environment. In short: our cognition of the world boils down to building a structure in which we define the pattern of relationships, mechanisms, and behaviors of a given entity—whether it's an organization, an IT system, or the way we create content.

We constantly try to fit the models built in our heads to the reality around us. This helps us navigate unusual situations efficiently by reducing our immediate cognitive load. However, entering a space that completely mismatches our internal model requires additional energetic effort. No wonder we evolutionarily prefer familiar paths and schemas—a mechanism successfully exploited by retail chains and fast-food giants when designing repeatable customer experiences.

## Knowledge Transfer and Architectural Structures

This exact same mechanics applies when exploring new areas of knowledge. Every book, even those seemingly distant from our daily specialization, can provide us with a unique mental model that turns out to be a breakthrough in our industry. This is why spot-on comparisons, metaphorical images, and anecdotes resonate so strongly with us. They allow us to transplant a proven structure from one domain to another.

I genuinely experience this while juggling different hats in my daily work. I have to trigger a completely different mental model when designing business requirements or preparing precise contract attachments, another when writing short-form posts for social media, and yet another when constructing complex architectural presentations. It is not just the vocabulary that changes, but the entire narrative, the way sentences are built, and the granularity of the structure. In such moments, my brain literally switches between distinct execution contexts.

## Reflection and Further Development

The key takeaways from this observation are simple, yet fundamental for the work of an analyst and architect:

* **Exploring horizons:** It is highly valuable to learn things outside your immediate specialization. New domains provide unconventional models that work exceptionally well in solving complex engineering problems.
* **Awareness of limitations:** We must constantly remember that the human brain naturally strives for simplifications and closing known patterns, which do not necessarily reflect reality accurately.
